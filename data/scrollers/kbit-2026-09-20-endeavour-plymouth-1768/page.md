## Tagline

A scientific voyage to measure Venus also carried sealed orders that reshaped Pacific maps and empire.

## Overview

HM Bark Endeavour left Plymouth in August 1768 under Lieutenant James Cook. Its public scientific objective was to reach Tahiti and observe the 1769 transit of Venus, part of an international effort to improve estimates of the Earth–Sun distance. The expedition also carried Joseph Banks, Daniel Solander, astronomers and artists who gathered extensive scientific observations and specimens.

After the Tahiti observation, Cook opened sealed Admiralty instructions connected to the search for the supposed southern continent, Terra Australis. The voyage went on to circumnavigate and chart New Zealand and chart the east coast of Australia. It therefore sits at the intersection of astronomy, navigation, natural history, cartography and British imperial expansion.

A useful detail: Cook's nautical journal dates the departure **26 August 1768**, while Joseph Banks recorded the sailing as **25 August** using civil time. Eighteenth-century naval timekeeping accounts for the apparent one-day disagreement.

## Why it matters

One departure from Plymouth links several major eighteenth-century stories: better measurement of the solar system, increasingly precise global navigation, systematic scientific collecting, European mapping of the Pacific, encounters with Indigenous peoples, and the territorial ambitions of the British Empire.
